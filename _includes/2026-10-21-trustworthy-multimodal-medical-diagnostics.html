Event Overview: Trustworthy Multimodal Medical Diagnostics — From Innovation to Adoption

Multimodal AI can bring together imaging, clinical data, text and other sources to support medical diagnosis. But when does adding another source actually improve a clinical decision? At AdSoLve’s second workshop on multimodal medical diagnostics, held on 21 October 2026 at The Alan Turing Institute in London, researchers, clinicians and health technology stakeholders explored that question through chest X-ray reporting, hospital risk prediction and cancer prevention. The closing panel considered what it would take for the NHS to adopt, govern and monitor these tools in routine care.

Professor Maria Liakata opened the day by placing that question within AdSoLve’s work on the socio-technical limitations of large language models (LLMs) in medicine, mental health support and law. AdSoLve works with stakeholders to develop benchmarks, metrics and transparent ways to evaluate LLM outputs. It also designs methods with domain experts to address limitations in temporal reasoning and longitudinal modelling. Radiology reporting and longitudinal monitoring illustrate why these limitations matter: clinical data can be hard to access and link, some sources are missing or sparse, and models can generate unsupported findings or struggle to reason across visits. These are concrete starting points for deciding what to evaluate.

Professor Greg Slabaugh showed how imaging, clinical notes and molecular data can reveal different aspects of a patient’s condition across visits. Drawing on feedback from the previous workshop, he argued for starting with a clinical decision and testing whether additional data improve on a strong baseline that uses a single source, while checking how systems cope with missing inputs and mismatched time points. He highlighted longitudinal cancer datasets, ultrasound foundation models, diagnostic agents that leave an auditable record, and early work on modelling how a patient’s condition might evolve. Across these examples, he asked whether the systems improve clinical decisions once verification time, workload and cost are accounted for, and how reliability can be monitored while clinicians retain oversight. Greg Slabaugh’s slides (PDF).

Cian Kennedy presented chest X-ray models for tasks such as generating reports from images. He showed that a training objective that accounts for relationships between diseases could improve tasks that connect images and text by organising model representations into ‘clinical neighbourhoods’, even though those representations appeared less clearly clustered.

Guneet Kohli showed how a reference-free method that represents reasoning steps as a graph can flag claims that lack supporting evidence in a text-based clinical case. He outlined how extending the method to multimodal consultations could help track where reasoning loses its grounding and distinguish a misread image from a flawed inference.

Mahmud Elahi Akhter demonstrated the AdSoLve evaluation platform using a chest X-ray reporting scenario informed by a radiologist. He traced the goal of streamlining report writing through requirements and tasks to measurable qualities such as clinical significance and conciseness. He showed how users can inspect individual reports and scores, including cases where an underspecified reference makes a score harder to interpret. Users can adapt an existing evaluation or build one around their own requirements.

Invited Talks

Kenza Bouzid (Microsoft) extended the discussion of chest X-ray reporting by looking inside MAIRA-2. She explained how a report can draw on several views, describe changes since a prior study and record the absence of clinically relevant findings. Using sparse autoencoders, she identified features inside the model and tested how adjusting them changed its reports. In one hand-picked example, an adjustment removed unsupported comparisons with prior studies. In another, steering the model to mention pleural effusion also introduced atelectasis. Bouzid noted that the study involved manual tuning. She also described how the team used human-annotated reports to refine prompts for an LLM judge. Bounding boxes could show radiologists where the model located a finding on the X-ray, although human evaluation of those boxes was limited.

Dr Jonathan Pearson (NHS England) focused on what clinicians can learn from combining data sources to predict hospital outcomes. Presenting early work with MIMIC data on prolonged hospital stays, in-hospital death, ICU admission and mechanical ventilation, he showed that simply combining inputs gave a strong baseline. More complex methods improved predictions for some deterioration outcomes. Simple summaries of irregularly sampled measurements helped, while the methods tested for filling in missing data added little. Pearson noted that a missing measurement can reflect a clinical decision, such as whether to order an X-ray. No method for combining data consistently improved fairness, and reducing disparities for one patient characteristic could worsen them for another. He urged researchers to check how clinicians interpret model explanations, since a convincing visual can inspire more confidence than the evidence warrants. Jonathan Pearson’s slides (PDF).

Lara Edwards (Health Data Research UK) described the Cancer Data Driven Detection (CD3) programme, which aims to improve cancer risk prediction and guide screening using health records, administrative data and longitudinal cohorts. A Welsh pilot linked cancer service records with census data and found lower screening participation in more deprived areas. CD3 has since built linked cohorts in Wales and plans to replicate the approach across the other UK nations, where coding practices and rules for AI research vary. Edwards highlighted how population-level data can include groups underrepresented in cohorts that rely on individual consent. She stressed working with data providers, patients and the public. With few published cancer risk models reaching routine care, she urged involving clinicians and electronic health record developers from the outset. Lara Edwards’ slides (PDF).

Breakout Discussions

Groups explored what evidence the NHS needs to use ambient voice technologies and multimodal imaging tools in practice. Participants saw room to improve existing tools and explore new applications, but pointed to limited evidence about how reliably systems handle different datasets and how their outputs can be assessed. The discussions identified ways that research could address these barriers. AdSoLve is planning a publication on the research priorities and evidence needs raised in the session.

Panel Discussion: What Would It Take for the NHS to Adopt These Tools?

Professor Matthew Purver asked whether the NHS should use AI more widely and what risks might arise even when it worked. David Seymour saw scope to expand capacity and improve access, while warning of disruption to services built on ageing infrastructure. Dr Nick de Pennington argued that AI could take on some work currently done by clinicians to extend access to care. Dr Raquel Iniesta said those decisions should be made task by task, with ongoing monitoring and care to preserve the clinician–patient relationship.

On what slows adoption, Dr Usman Bashir described spending years securing access to a small amount of NHS data. De Pennington pointed to weak incentives for staff who approve new tools, while Iniesta highlighted the difficulty of reconciling overlapping rules. From the audience, Professor Tom Sorell noted that regulation reflects competing public interests. Gian Marco Strappelli called for shared guidance to help NHS trusts apply the rules.

Purver asked what developers could do. Seymour urged teams to evaluate tools against current clinical practice. Iniesta asked whether adding more data would improve care enough to justify collecting it. Strappelli suggested giving clinicians a way to flag whether they agreed with a tool’s findings so that teams could monitor performance after deployment. Bashir proposed testing models across institutions.

Asked whether global trade could help scale healthcare AI, de Pennington stressed that tools must fit local expectations of care. Maria Liakata then asked how junior clinicians would develop the judgement needed for difficult decisions if AI took over routine tasks. Iniesta emphasised the tacit knowledge gained through practice, while Bashir argued that clinicians can adapt as tools change.

Closing Reflections

Across the day, the value of combining data came down to two questions: does the extra information improve a clinical decision, and can clinicians check how the model used it? Answering those questions means comparing tools with current clinical practice, testing them across institutions and monitoring how they perform once in use.
