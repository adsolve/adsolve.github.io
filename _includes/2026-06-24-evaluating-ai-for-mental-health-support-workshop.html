Event Overview: Evaluating AI for mental health support - AdSoLve Stakeholder Workshop

The AdSoLve Co-Creation Workshop on Evaluating AI for Mental Health took place on Wednesday 24 June 2026 at the Alan Turing Institute, London. The day brought together presentations from the AdSoLve team, invited talks from external experts on AI applications in mental health, a panel discussion on key considerations for evaluating and implementing AI-assisted tools in mental health care, and an evaluation consensus workshop focused on the socio-technical evaluation of LLMs in this setting.

AdSoLve Team Presentations

Prof. Maria Liakata (QMUL) presented an overview of AdSoLve, a multidisciplinary project spanning Queen Mary, Nottingham, Sheffield, Warwick, and the Responsible AI UK network. The project brings together expertise in NLP, law, ethics, and clinical psychiatry to tackle the socio-technical limitations of LLMs in medicine and law. It pursues two goals: building multi-faceted evaluation methods, including novel benchmarks, metrics, and a transparent reference-free approach beyond LLM-as-a-judge for assessing LLM limitations in real-world settings; and developing new machine-learning solutions, co-created with domain experts, for temporal reasoning, situational awareness, bias reduction, and privacy preservation. She grounded the work in growing evidence of the risks of AI chatbots as mental health support tools, citing reports of over-reliance, inappropriate advice, and sycophantic behaviour, alongside persistent NLP challenges such as hallucination, weak longitudinal reasoning, poor explainability, and privacy leakage. She also provided an overview of AdSoLve's evaluation platform, which combines benchmarks, LLM-as-a-judge, and transparent reference-free reasoning-based evaluation. Presentation slides.

Talia Tseriotou (QMUL) introduced her work on TempoFormer (Tseriotou et al., 2024), a transformer that adapts only its top layers through temporal embeddings that capture the distance between posts in chronologically ordered streams. Its efficacy has been shown on three change-detection tasks with different temporal granularities, including mood changes such as switches and escalations in longitudinal social media data. It outperformed few-shot prompted LLMs, exposing their persistent struggles with temporal reasoning over irregular, evolving, long-range context. This was followed by a presentation on the CLPsych Shared Task (Ali et al., 2026a), in which global teams competed to detect and summarise psychological state changes in social media timelines and where TempoFormer remains highly competitive. With a record number of teams competing this year, results showed that maladaptive states escalate monotonically with presence intensity, whereas adaptive states peak at moderate presence before declining. These findings surface distinct behavioural trajectories that could help anticipate deterioration or improvement early.

Talia then presented work with Iqra Ali (QMUL) on enabling in-context learning in LLMs for longitudinal tasks, where current models fail to capture structure in long, longitudinally evolving context. The introduced LiFT method (Ali et al., 2026b) circumvents these limitations through an instruction fine-tuning framework based on staged curriculum learning and temporal-label conditioning. This teaches models to attend to history in long-form documents and generalise to out-of-distribution tasks involving predictions over time, irrespective of temporal granularity. Presentation slides.

Prof. Domenico Giacco (University of Warwick) delivered a talk on AI in Mental Health Systems: Early Phase Evaluation of AI-DIALOG+. He gave a brief overview of DIALOG+, presented in more detail later by Stefan Priebe, and explored how adding AI could help it reach more people without replacing clinicians, thereby enhancing evidence-based treatment provision within mental health services. Drawing on workshops in which patients and clinicians tested a preliminary AI-assisted version, he outlined what matters most for making such tools work well in practice: they must add real value, fit naturally into existing care, remain accessible and inclusive, and, above all, be safe, with clear oversight and risk pathways. Within AdSoLve, we plan to develop an improved version of AI-DIALOG+ based on open-source LLMs and guided by further user-based workshops. Presentation slides.

Dr Aislinn Bergin (University of Nottingham) introduced AdSoLve's framework for evaluating LLMs in high-stakes domains such as healthcare and law, built on three linked elements: requirements, aspects, and metrics. The approach shifts evaluation away from technical performance alone and towards real-world stakeholder needs, using structured stakeholder engagement and a consensus process to produce evaluation specifications that are both measurable and meaningful to the people who rely on them. Presentation slides.

Prof. Rob Procter (University of Warwick) extended the evaluation question into lifecycle-long "assurance", arguing that AI evaluation should not stop at procurement. Citing evidence that over 95% of organisations adopting AI have not reported productivity gains, he highlighted the concept of "workslop", where AI speeds up part of a workflow but produces output below the required standard, creating rework downstream. He argued that the unit of evaluation should be the workflow with AI embedded rather than AI in isolation, since both the system and how people use it can change over time. A social care case study showed workers already treating AI drafts as a scaffold to edit and take ownership of, although this assurance work is largely informal and best practice has yet to emerge across social care teams. Presentation slides.

Sebastian Löbbers (QMUL) presented AdSoLve's evaluation platform, focusing on the mental health use case. The platform provides multi-faceted evaluation through a range of metrics as well as reference-free evaluation that goes beyond LLM-as-a-judge. He discussed the progression from stakeholder goals to requirements, tasks, aspects, and metrics, so that abstract real-world needs become computationally measurable. The platform allows users either to browse existing evaluation bundles or to build an evaluation pipeline from a library of tasks, aspects, and metrics. He illustrated this with an NHS Trust scenario in which psychologists use LLMs to summarise clients' social media posts, deriving requirements such as privacy and factual accuracy that map to aspects assessed by multiple metrics, since no single metric is considered sufficient. He also used the demonstration as a starting point for discussing how evidence provided by the platform can translate into regulatory compliance. Presentation slides.

Invited Talks

Stefan Priebe (University of Hamburg) presented DIALOG+, a structured tool that turns routine mental health check-ins into effective therapy in their own right. Patients rate satisfaction across key life areas, then choose what to address using a simple four-step process, with most resulting actions set by the patient rather than the clinician. A randomised trial found that DIALOG+ improved quality of life, with effects at least as large as CBT, reduced symptoms, improved social outcomes, and cut costs. These benefits were linked to how it addresses patient concerns, supports problem-solving, and empowers patients to act. Now tested in more than 18 countries and freely available online, DIALOG+ points towards a more flexible, patient-driven future for mental health support, including through AI. Presentation slides.

Sam Stern (Affiniti AI) described a blended approach that keeps human elements such as the therapeutic alliance with the clinician, while AI handles psychoeducation and skills practice under clinical direction. His core insight, supported by both a hospital prescribing case study and Affiniti's own data, is that risk and disengagement rarely arise from a single flawed output. Instead, they emerge in the handoffs, missed check-ins, and continuity gaps between interactions, where operational failures are experienced by patients as relational abandonment. He called for expanding evaluation beyond individual responses to cover units ranging from entire conversations to multi-session interventions and care pathways. Presentation slides.

Antonia Vehlen (Trier University) shared how AI is being used to move beyond questionnaire-based therapy monitoring towards richer, AI-driven clinical support. She presented several tools used at Wolfgang Lutz's clinic at the University of Trier. The Trier Treatment Navigator gives therapists individualised treatment recommendations and alerts them when patients drift off track. A large trial showed that the feedback improves outcomes only when therapists actually use it, prompting the team to make the tools easier to adopt and to add new data sources such as session recordings. She described three projects illustrating this shift: LLM-based rating scales that infer clinical measures directly from session transcripts; multimodal analyses testing whether combining video, audio, and human-annotated data can improve prediction of patient outcomes beyond text alone; and PsyRAI, an AI rater that scores therapists' micro-level clinical skills and gives trainee psychologists direct feedback on their responses. She closed by noting that AI can help clinicians measure, understand, and train better, but only if it fits into real clinical practice. Presentation slides.

Panel Discussion: Key Considerations for Evaluation and Implementation of AI-Assisted Tools in Mental Health Care

The panel brought together Dr Bev Brown (Senior Project and Implementation Manager at the Institute of Mental Health at Nottingham University), Dr Edward Millgate (Clinical Engineer at Scarlet), Sam Pye (Trainee Clinical Psychologist at Surrey and Borders Partnership NHS Foundation Trust), and Mat Rawsthorne (Lived Experience Advisory Panel member at Generation Mental Health). It was chaired by Prof. Rob Procter.

Questions were put to the panel on the following topics:

  • What evidence is needed to trust and adopt Large Language Models in mental health?
  • How do we ensure that benefits are maximised and risks mitigated when Large Language Models are used in practice?
  • How is the adoption of Large Language Models in mental health practice likely to change mental health service provision over the next five years?

Panellists agreed that randomised controlled trials move too slowly for AI, since a trial can take years while models change within months. Platform trials, which swap interventions in and out efficiently, were suggested instead, alongside evaluation tied to a device's actual intended purpose. Trust was described as requiring continuous assurance rather than a one-time certificate, together with feedback grounded in lived experience of using the tools, since strong performance on paper does not guarantee a good real-world experience for the client.

Panellists pointed to a regulatory gap between general-purpose LLMs and certified clinical decision-support tools, since the line between a brainstorming aid and a clinical decision-making tool can be crossed with a single prompt. Professional deskilling was raised as an under-discussed risk, alongside epistemic injustice, where a recorded state can become a fixed trait overnight with no way to challenge it. Strong post-market systems and tracking of performance drift were considered essential once devices enter real-world use, while accountability for harm remains an ongoing concern.

For the next five years, panellists identified clearer regulatory classification as necessary groundwork, with stakeholder buy-in and clinician acceptance likely to remain barriers. Automation bias was flagged as a risk: a tool assumed to be right most of the time can still be wrong once, with significant consequences. NHS pressure to shorten waiting times was also seen as being in tension with practitioners' aim to treat patients well rather than merely quickly. The discussion closed on the idea that the field already has more innovation than it can implement, so the task ahead is to build infrastructure that supports patient autonomy in the gap between referral and treatment.

Evaluation Consensus Workshop

The Evaluation Consensus Workshop brought together participants across three breakout groups to discuss how stakeholder requirements can be translated into socio-technical evaluation metrics and to build consensus on proposed evaluation packages. Discussions were grounded in two scenarios: DIALOG+, used by secondary care patients between care coordinator appointments, and a summarisation tool used by primary care practitioners to draft therapy-session notes. Participants agreed that the high-level requirements were broadly sound, but identified key gaps, including the need for a basic factuality check; greater linguistic and cultural specificity when judging appropriateness and empathy; better handling of layered emotions in complex conversations; and more realistic measures of conversational flow for therapy settings, where resolution is often subjective. Safety and boundary crossing were seen as harder to define objectively than capability limits, pointing to a need for clinician consensus rather than purely technical definitions.

On the proposed evaluation packages, participants broadly agreed with the core requirements and aspects but called for targeted modifications. Key recommendations included clearer categorisation of hallucination types with different tolerance levels, attribution of statements to the therapist or patient, support for conditional summaries relevant to a specific treatment, and stronger safeguards to ensure that AI never overrides clinician decision-making. Participants also emphasised emotional acknowledgement over emotional matching, selective flagging of concerns to clinicians rather than over-flagging, sensitivity to tone and cultural nuance, and avoiding immediate responses to emotionally significant disclosures.

Closing Reflections

By the end of the day, several key takeaways had emerged. AI tools show real potential in mental health care, but no single metric or one-off evaluation can capture whether they are safe and trustworthy in practice. Assurance needs to be continuous, tied to real clinical workflows, and built with clinicians, patients, and lived experience at the centre, not merely around technical benchmarks. AdSoLve's work aims to build evaluation frameworks that reflect this while continuing to develop solutions that address LLMs' socio-technical limitations, so that clinicians and patients have the evidence they need to build trust in these systems.
